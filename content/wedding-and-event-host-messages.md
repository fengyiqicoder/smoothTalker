# Wedding and event host messages

## Goal
Guests know exactly what is expected, feel welcome, and do not feel singled out by the limits. Rules for a big event are fine; they land well when they are stated once, warmly, for everyone, before anyone has to ask.

Declining an invitation you received: `decline-invitation`. A group chat for planning: `group-chat-coordination`.

## Structure
1. **Put the rule in the invitation or website**, framed as a fact about the event ("an adults-only celebration"), not a list of banned people.
2. **When someone asks for an exception, thank them for asking.**
3. **State the limit as applying to everyone**, with the plain reason in a few words (space, budget, numbers).
4. **Add a warm line about them** and what you are looking forward to.
5. **No negotiation.** One exception becomes five; decide in advance who, if anyone, is exempt.

## Principles
- A rule for everyone is easy to accept. A rule that seems aimed at one person is not, so apply limits by category (no plus-ones unless engaged or living together), and keep to the category.
- Say it early. The invitation or website is the kindest place; a text a week before is the hardest.
- Gifts are always optional. Mention preferences only where guests will look (website, card insert), never as a demand.
- RSVP chasers are admin. Keep them short and give a real date and the reason (the caterer needs numbers).
- Uninviting is rare and serious. Do it in person or by call if you can, give the real reason briefly, and take the cost on yourself.
- Keep your partner or co-host aligned. Contradictory answers from two hosts are how guest-list feuds start.

## Examples

**Invitation wording, no plus-ones:**
> We've kept the guest list small so we can celebrate with the people closest to us. Your invitation is for the names on the envelope; we hope you understand.

**Invitation wording, adults only:**
> We love your little ones, but we've chosen to make this an adults-only celebration. We hope it's a chance for a night off.

**Someone asks to bring a new partner:**
> Thanks so much for asking, and I'm glad things are going well with Sam. We've had to keep plus-ones to partners who live together to stay within numbers, so it's just you this time. I can't wait to see you there.

**Gifts, card insert or website:**
> Your company is the best gift. If you'd like to give something, a contribution towards our honeymoon would mean a lot, and there are details on the website.

**Dress code, clear and specific:**
> Dress code: cocktail. Think suits without ties, dresses or smart separates. The ceremony is on grass, so block heels or flats are a good idea.

**RSVP chaser:**
> Hi Laura, just chasing RSVPs for the 14th. Could you let me know by Friday if you can make it, and any dietary needs? The caterer needs final numbers on Monday.

**Cutting the guest list before invites go out (to someone expecting one):**
> I wanted to tell you myself rather than you wonder: we've had to cut the wedding right down to family and a few friends, so we couldn't invite everyone we'd hoped to, including you. I'm really sorry, and I'd love to take you for dinner when we're back.

**Uninviting after invitations went out (rare, by call if possible):**
> I owe you a hard message. After what happened with Mia, we've decided it's best that you don't come to the wedding. I know that's painful to hear, and I didn't make the decision lightly. I'd rather tell you straight than leave it unsaid.

## Avoid
- "No ring, no bring" and other jokes that make the rule feel like a jab.
- Listing names of people who are or are not allowed.
- Putting gift preferences on the main invitation card, or asking for cash as an expectation.
- Making an exception for one guest and hoping nobody notices.
- Uninviting by group message, or blaming it on "the budget" when it is a personal reason.

## If it goes badly
If a guest pushes back on a limit, repeat it once, shorter, and do not add new reasons; new reasons are new targets. If they say they will not come without their partner or children, accept it warmly and do not bargain: "I understand, we'll miss you." If family members start lobbying for someone, agree a single answer with your co-host and let one of you give it. If an uninvited guest threatens to turn up, tell the venue and a trusted person to handle it quietly on the day, and do not argue it again in writing.
> I get it, and I'm sorry it's disappointing. We're keeping it the same for everyone. I really hope you'll still come.
> That's completely your call, and we'll miss you. Let's celebrate properly together when things calm down.
