# Mediating between two people

## Goal
The two people talk to each other instead of through you, and you keep both relationships. You are not the judge and not the messenger. Your job is to lower the temperature and make a direct conversation easier; whether they make up is theirs.

Your own argument with someone: `de-escalate-argument`. Giving one of them feedback as their manager: `give-feedback-to-colleague`.

## Structure
1. **Acknowledge each person privately**, one at a time. Hearing them is not agreeing with them.
2. **Say plainly that you will not take sides or pass messages.** Kindly, once, early.
3. **Suggest they talk directly**, and offer to help set it up if they want that.
4. **If you are asked to be in the room, set simple ground rules**: one topic, each speaks without interruption, aim for one agreement.
5. **Step back.** Check in once later, and leave the outcome to them.

## Principles
- Neutral does not mean silent. You can care about both people and still refuse to rule on who is right.
- Do not relay what one said about the other. Messages carried by a third person get sharper on the way.
- Keep confidences. What A told you in private is not yours to use to persuade B.
- Look for the shared goal: the friendship, the project, the family event. People agree on the goal long before they agree on the facts.
- As a manager, you are not neutral about behaviour. You can stay neutral on the personal dispute and still set clear expectations about how people treat each other at work.
- Your job has limits. If there is abuse, harassment or a safety issue, it is not a mediation; point them to the right help (HR, a professional, or authorities).

## Examples

**Friend venting about the other friend:**
> That sounds really hurtful, and I'm glad you told me. I care about you both, so I'm not going to take sides or pass anything on. I do think it's worth saying this to Jess directly. Want to talk through how you'd put it?

**Declining to be the messenger:**
> I'd rather not carry messages between you two, it'll only get muddled. If you want her to know that, it's better coming from you. I'm happy to help you word it.

**Being asked to pick a side:**
> I'm not going to pick a side, because I don't think it would help either of you. I'm still your friend, and I'll still be hers. I'd really like you two to talk.

**Offering to host a conversation (friends or family):**
> I know things are tense between you and Mia. Would you both be up for a coffee with me there? No ambush, I'll tell her the same thing. Just a chance to hear each other out.

**Ground rules at the start of a joint conversation:**
> Thanks for coming, both of you. Let's keep it to the trip money, one person talks at a time, and let's try to leave with one thing we agree on.

**Manager, two teammates clashing (to each, privately):**
> I've noticed things have been tense between you and Sam, especially in the release meetings. I'm not here to decide who's right. I need the two of you to find a way to work together, and I'd like to sit down with both of you on Thursday to agree how handoffs will work. Anything you want me to know before then?

**Manager, setting expectations after the joint meeting:**
> Thanks both. To confirm what we agreed: Sam sends the build notes by 3pm, Alex reviews by noon the next day, and disagreements come to me rather than going into the team channel. Let's check in again in two weeks.

## Avoid
- "Well, she told me you said..."
- Summarising each person's case to the other.
- Taking a side quietly while claiming to be neutral.
- Forcing a reconciliation on a timetable, or a group hug in public.
- Discussing the conflict with mutual friends or the wider team.
- As a manager, waiting until it spills into the team channel.

## If it goes badly
If one person refuses to talk to the other, respect it and stop pushing; say the offer stands and leave it there. If either one turns on you for not taking their side, stay calm and repeat that you care about both of them. If a joint conversation blows up, pause it kindly and suggest trying again another day, or with a neutral professional such as an HR partner, a counsellor or a community mediator. At work, if the conflict keeps affecting the team, it becomes a performance matter; handle it with each person individually.
> I get that you wanted me on your side, and I'm sorry that's hard. I'm still your friend. That hasn't changed.
> Let's stop here for today. I don't think we'll get further right now, and that's okay. Can we try again next week?
