# Declining a meeting or protecting your time

## Goal
The meeting does not happen, or happens smaller, and the organiser still gets what they wanted from you. Nobody feels dismissed.

## Structure
1. **Name what they need**, not the meeting. "You need a decision on the vendor" or "you want my read on the draft."
2. **Decline the format, not the person.** "I'll skip the call" is about the call.
3. **Give them the thing another way**: a written answer, a shorter slot, a named colleague, a date.
4. **One line, no defence.** Reasons about the calendar invite debate; a plain alternative does not.
5. **Close with the yes**: "does that work?" or "I'll send it by 3."

## Principles
- Replace, don't reject. A decline with a working alternative is a favour to the organiser, because it gets them the outcome faster.
- A meeting with no agenda is a question. Ask for the question, then answer it.
- Juniors: decline the format and offer the deliverable. Do not say you are busy; say what you will send and when.
- Seniors: say no plainly. Your no needs less padding than you think, and padding reads as indecision.
- Recurring meetings die quietly. Nobody is offended when you leave one with a clean handover of what you contributed.
- Strangers and vendors asking for "15 minutes" are asking for your attention, not your time. Written answers cost them effort, which is the filter.
- Same-day requests are usually habit, not urgency. Ask once whether it truly cannot wait; most cannot answer yes.

## Examples

**Declining an invite, peer to peer:**
> I'll skip this one. If you need my input on the pricing page, I can comment on the doc by Thursday morning. Does that cover it?

**Proposing async instead, to a senior colleague:**
> Happy to help on the Q4 forecast. Rather than a meeting, I can send you my numbers and the three assumptions behind them by end of day tomorrow, and we can do a 10-minute call only if something doesn't add up. Would that work for you?

**Shortening a meeting (you are the organiser or a key attendee):**
> Could we do this as 20 minutes instead of an hour? The only open point is the launch date; if everyone reads the two-pager first, we should get through it fast. I've updated the invite.

**Leaving a recurring meeting, junior to manager:**
> I'd like to drop out of the Tuesday sync from next week. My part is now covered by the status doc, which I'll keep updated before each meeting. If a topic needs me, ping me and I'll join for that slot. Okay with you?

**Leaving a recurring meeting, senior to peers:**
> I'm stepping out of the weekly ops review. Lena has full context and will represent our side. Reach me directly if something needs my sign-off.

**Saying no to "quick call?" from a stranger or vendor:**
> Thanks for reaching out. I don't take intro calls, but if you send a short note on what it does and rough pricing, I'll read it and reply if it fits what we're working on.

**Pushing a same-day request to tomorrow:**
> I can get this to you by 10am tomorrow with the numbers checked. If you need something today, I can send a rough version by 4, unchecked. Which do you prefer?

**Declining a meeting from your own manager, offering the substance:**
> I can't make 3pm today. I've put my update and the two decisions I need from you in [doc]; if you drop your answers there, I'll move on it this afternoon and we can skip the call.

## Avoid
- "I'm swamped" or "my calendar is crazy". Everyone's is; it invites "just 15 minutes".
- Declining without saying what you will do instead. That is a wall, not an alternative.
- Accepting and then not turning up. Decline early; a no on the day costs more than a no now.
- Explaining your focus-time philosophy. Nobody asked, and it sounds superior.
- "Let me check and get back to you" when you already know it is a no.
- Silently ignoring a stranger's third follow-up when one clear "not now" would end it.

## If they push back
When they insist it has to be a meeting:
> Sure, let's do it. Can you send me the one decision you need from me so I come prepared, and let's cap it at 20 minutes?
When a vendor keeps chasing:
> This isn't something we're looking at this year. Please take me off the follow-up list; if that changes, I'll get in touch.
When your manager says the meeting matters:
> Understood, I'll be there. To make it worth the hour, can we put the agenda in the invite?
Then attend, contribute, and raise the format question again after two more sessions, with evidence.
