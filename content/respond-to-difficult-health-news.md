# Responding to difficult health news

## Goal
The person feels heard, not managed. They should not have to reassure you, educate you, or answer a list of questions. Your reply says three things: I heard you, I care, and here is something concrete I can do, with no need to reply.

After a death, use `condolences-and-support`. For someone who has gone quiet during a long hard time, `check-in-on-someone-struggling`.

## Structure
1. **Acknowledge the news plainly.** "I'm so sorry you're dealing with this" is enough. Do not look for the perfect line.
2. **Thank them for telling you**, if they told you directly. It often took effort.
3. **Follow their tone.** If they wrote it matter-of-factly or with a joke, you can be lighter. If they were scared, be steady.
4. **One specific offer**, or a standing one with a date.
5. **Release them from replying.**

## Principles
- It is their news to share. Do not pass it on, and do not ask "who else knows?"
- Questions about prognosis, stage, survival odds or cause are for them to volunteer, not for you to ask. "How did you find out?" and "Did you smoke?" both land as judgement.
- No stories about someone else with the same illness, especially ones that ended badly.
- No cures, diets, articles or "have you tried". Their doctors have this; you are there for the person.
- Avoid "everything happens for a reason" and "stay positive". They make fear feel like a failure.
- Keep showing up. Messages dry up after the first week, which is when the hard part usually starts. Set a reminder to check in again.
- A colleague's news at work stays professional: warmth plus practical flexibility, and privacy about the details.

## Examples

**Close friend, just told you:**
> Oh, Jess. I'm so sorry. Thank you for telling me. I'm here for all of it, the scary bits and the normal bits. Can I bring dinner on Thursday? No need to reply tonight.

**Parent sharing test results:**
> Thank you for telling me straight, Mum. I'm coming up on Saturday, and I'd like to come with you to the next appointment if you'd like that. I love you.

**They told you with humour:**
> Only you could announce a tumour with a pun. I'm so sorry, and I'm fully on board for the gallows humour. Also on board for lifts to the hospital, just give me dates.

**Friend in hospital:**
> Just heard you're in. Thinking of you. I won't visit unless you say so, but I can feed the cat and bring whatever you need. Tell me one thing and I'll do it.

**Colleague told you privately:**
> Thank you for trusting me with that. I'm really sorry. Whatever you need on the work side, whether it's cover for appointments or just not being asked about it, tell me and I'll make it happen. It stays with me.

**Chronic illness, a friend explaining why they keep cancelling:**
> Thank you for telling me, that makes a lot of sense and I'm sorry it's been so hard. No explaining needed going forward. I'll keep inviting you, and "not today" is always a fine answer.

**Check-in a few weeks later:**
> Thinking of you today. No need to reply. I'm free Sunday afternoon if you'd like company, or a walk, or a film on the sofa.

## Avoid
- "I know how you feel."
- "My aunt had that and..."
- "Let me know if you need anything." Replace it with one real offer.
- A list of questions about the medical details.
- Sending articles, cures or supplement links.
- Making your shock the centre ("I can't stop crying, this is so hard for me").

## If it goes badly
If you said the wrong thing (asked about odds, told a bad story, sent advice), own it in one line and return to them: no long apology that makes them comfort you. If they do not reply for weeks, keep sending short, no-reply-needed messages; silence is usually exhaustion, not rejection. If they say they want space, respect it and ask if they would like you to check back in a month.
> I've been thinking about what I said about my aunt, and it wasn't what you needed. I'm sorry. I'm here, and I'll follow your lead.
> Still thinking of you. No need to reply, I'll keep sending the odd message unless you'd rather I didn't.
