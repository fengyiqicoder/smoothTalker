# Giving critical feedback at work

## Goal
The behaviour changes and the person still feels respected and backed. Feedback that is not acted on was not feedback, it was venting.

## Structure (for a conversation; write it out first)
1. **Ask for the conversation**: "Can I give you some feedback on X?" Consent opens ears.
2. **The specific observation.** What you saw, when. Not a pattern word like "always".
3. **The impact.** On the work, the team, the client. Not on your feelings unless relevant.
4. **Pause and ask.** "What's your read on that?" Then actually listen. There is often context.
5. **The ask.** What you want to see instead, concretely.
6. **Support and follow-up.** "What would help?" and a date to check in.

## Principles
- Private, always. Praise in public, correct in private.
- Soon. Within days of the thing, not at the quarterly review.
- One thing per conversation.
- Behaviour, not personality. "The report had three errors in the totals" not "you're careless."
- Do not sandwich with fake praise. Real positive context is fine; a manufactured compliment before the point is transparent.
- Say it plainly. Managers who hint are experienced as unclear, not kind.
- Written follow-up after the conversation, short and neutral.

## Examples

**Missed deadlines, report:**
> Can I give you some feedback? The last three deliverables have come in a day or two after the date we agreed. The effect is that Sana's team ends up scrambling and it's starting to show up in how they talk about us. What's going on from your side?
> [listen]
> Okay. Going forward I need the dates to hold, or a heads-up by the Wednesday before if something's slipping. What would make that easier?

**Peer who talks over people in meetings:**
> Hey, something I've noticed and I think you'd want to know: in the last couple of meetings you've jumped in over Maya and Lee a few times before they finished. I don't think you're doing it on purpose. It's meant a couple of good points got lost. Would you be up for leaving a beat after people finish? I'll do the same.

**Quality issue, senior person to junior:**
> The deck you sent had the wrong Q2 numbers on two slides and it went to the client before we caught it. That's the kind of thing we can't let out the door. I want to work out a check that catches it: what if you send me numbers slides an hour before anything external?

**Attitude / tone issue (hardest one):**
> I want to raise something that's a bit uncomfortable. In the planning meeting, when Tom suggested the phased approach, the response came across as dismissive, and I saw him shut down after that. I don't think that's what you intended. The effect is that people stop offering ideas. Can we talk about how that landed?

**Written follow-up:**
> Thanks for the conversation earlier. To capture it: we agreed deliverable dates hold, with a heads-up by the prior Wednesday if anything's at risk. Let's check in on the 20th. Let me know if I've missed anything.

## Avoid
- "Some people have said..."
- "You always / you never."
- Feedback by email when it should be a conversation.
- Piling several issues into one meeting.
- Ending without a clear ask.
- Not following up. Silence after feedback reads as "it didn't matter."
