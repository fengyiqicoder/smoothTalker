# Cross-cultural and multilingual notes

These are tendencies, not rules. Always weight the individual's own messages over their assumed background. The mirror rule in `02-tone-calibration` still comes first.

## The main dial: directness
Cultures differ most on how explicitly a no, a disagreement or a request is stated.

**Higher-directness defaults** (common in the US, Netherlands, Germany, Israel, Australia): a plain "no" or "I disagree" is normal and respected. Softening too much reads as evasive or unclear.

**Lower-directness defaults** (common in Japan, Korea, Thailand, Indonesia, much of the Middle East, often the UK and Canada in polite registers): a no is often implied ("that might be difficult", "let me think about it"). A blunt no can read as rude; a blunt disagreement can cause loss of face, especially in front of others.

**Practical adjustments**
- Writing to a lower-directness reader: add one sentence of acknowledgement before the point, offer the no as a difficulty or alternative rather than a flat refusal, and never disagree in a group message. Take it private.
- Writing to a higher-directness reader: cut hedges, put the ask in line one, and do not apologise for asking.
- Reading a message from a lower-directness sender: "we will consider it" and "it may be difficult" often mean no. Do not push for a harder answer publicly; ask privately and gently.

## Formality
- Some cultures expect titles and surnames until invited otherwise (Germany, Japan, Korea, much of Latin America in business). When unsure, start formal and mirror their reply.
- Age and seniority carry more weight in many East Asian, South Asian and African contexts. A junior person messaging a senior one should be warmer and more formal than they would be with a peer.
- Emoji, exclamation marks and first names are safe with peers almost everywhere under 40; be more careful with seniors and first contacts.

## Structure
- Some readers expect relationship first, business second (much of Latin America, the Middle East, South Asia): a line asking after them or their family is not filler, it is the message working. Skip it and you sound cold.
- Others expect business first (Germany, Nordics, US business email): the pleasantries can go after the point or be omitted.

## Writing to a non-native speaker
- Short sentences. One idea each.
- Avoid idioms, sarcasm and jokes that depend on wordplay.
- Say the ask plainly; indirect English ("you might want to consider") is often missed.
- Dates in unambiguous form: "14 March", not 3/14.

## When the user is writing in their second language
- Do not "correct" their voice into corporate English. Keep it simple and natural.
- Suggest the version that is easiest to say and to read, not the most sophisticated.
- If they ask for a translation of a diplomatic message, translate the intent, not the words; softeners do not map one to one.

## Avoid
- Stereotyping an individual from their name or location.
- Over-formality as a default; many people find it distancing.
- Assuming English-language norms of directness are neutral. They are not.
