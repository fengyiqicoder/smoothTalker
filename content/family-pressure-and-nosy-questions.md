# Family pressure and nosy questions

## Goal
The question stops without a scene. You keep your privacy, the relative keeps their dignity, and the table moves on. You are not trying to win, educate, or get them to agree that the question was rude.

## Structure
1. **Light acknowledgement.** A smile, a laugh, "ah, the classic."
2. **Short non-answer.** "When there's news, you'll be the first to know."
3. **Redirect immediately** to something they like talking about. The redirect is the deflection; steps 1 and 2 are just the runway.
4. **If it comes back a second time**, repeat the same non-answer, nearly word for word. Consistency ends it. Variation invites a new angle.
5. **If it comes back a third time, or with a dig attached**, move to the firmer version below.

## Firmer version, for repeat offenders
1. **Name it as a topic, not a person.** "This one is off the table."
2. **Say what you will do.** "If it comes up again I'll go help in the kitchen."
3. **Follow through** the next time, calmly, no announcement.
4. **Stay warm on everything else.** The boundary is about one subject, not the relationship.

## Principles
- Do not explain. A reason ("we're trying, actually", "I'm saving") is an invitation to advise.
- Humour works once. Repeated jokes read as nerves and keep the topic alive.
- The same sentence every time. You are teaching them that the door does not open.
- You can be private without being cold. Most relatives ask because they care, badly.
- A cross-cultural note: in many families, a soft deflection is not avoidance, it is the normal and respectful way to close a topic, and a stated hard boundary can land as a rupture. If that is your family, treat deflection as the whole playbook and keep the firmer version as a last resort for someone who is genuinely hurting you, not someone who is merely annoying.

## Examples

**"When are you getting married?":**
> Ha, you and my mother should form a committee. When there's news, you'll hear it first. Now tell me about the garden, Dad said you finally beat the slugs.

**"When are you having kids?" (possibly painful, keep it short):**
> That's between me and [partner], but I love that you're keen. Have you seen the photos from [cousin]'s trip? Pass me your phone.

**Career, "still doing that job?":**
> Still doing it, still like it. Not much to report. How's the knee, did the physio help?

**Weight or appearance:**
> I'm happy and healthy, and I'm not discussing my body at dinner. Can you pass the potatoes?

**Money, "how much are you making now?":**
> Enough to pay for lunch next time, which I'll do. How did the house sale go in the end?

**Religion, "you'll come back to church eventually":**
> I know it matters to you, and I'm grateful for that. It's not something I'm going to debate. Are you still doing the Tuesday choir?

**Moving home, "when are you coming back for good?":**
> I'm settled where I am for now, and I'll always come for the holidays. Let's make the summer one longer. When does the school break start for the kids?

**Firmer version, by text after the third time:**
> I know you mean well, and I need you to stop asking about [kids / marriage / my weight]. It's off the table. I'd love to talk about anything else on Sunday. See you at 1.

## Avoid
- Defending your choice with facts and studies. You are not on trial.
- "It's none of your business." True, and it makes the whole table tense. Save the sharp version for someone who has earned it.
- Recruiting another relative to fight for you at the table. Handle it yourself, in one line, and let it drop.
- Pre-empting the question in a long group message before the holiday. It usually makes things worse.
- Answering the subtext ("you think I've failed") instead of the words.

## If they push back
If the relative sulks, escalates or says you have changed, hold the line and stay kind:
> I'm not upset with you and I'm not going to talk about that one. Come help me with the dishes.

If a parent takes the firmer version as rejection, address the relationship, not the topic:
> Mum, I love you and I want the time we have together to be good. That's exactly why I'm asking for this one subject to rest.

If a relative is deliberately cruel rather than clumsy, you are allowed to leave the room. One line as you go, no explanation:
> I'm going to step out for a bit. Back in ten.
