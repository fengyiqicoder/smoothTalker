# Asking for a review, testimonial or referral

## Structure
1. **Thank them for something specific** about the purchase or the project.
2. **The ask, framed by its effect**: what a review does for a small business.
3. **One link and a time estimate.** "Two sentences, about a minute."
4. **A genuine out.** "Thank you either way."
5. **For testimonials, a draft they can edit** so the work is already done.

## Goal
The customer does it in the next minute because it's easy and because they liked you. Nobody feels pestered.

## Principles
- **Timing:** ask right after a good moment (delivery arrived, project shipped, they said thanks). Not weeks later.
- **One link, one platform.** Do not send three links.
- **Tell them it's short.** "Two sentences is plenty."
- **Make the ask about the effect**, not about you: "reviews are how small shops get found."
- **Offer to draft** for testimonials; most people say yes and edit.
- **Never incentivise reviews** on platforms that ban it (Google, most marketplaces). A thank-you after is fine.
- **Ask once, remind once.** Then stop.

## Examples

**After a delivery, DM or email:**
> So glad the lamp arrived safely! If you have a minute, a quick Google review would honestly make a difference; it's how people find us. Here's the link: [link]. Two sentences is plenty. Thank you either way.

**After a service (hair, cleaning, repair):**
> Thanks for coming in today, it was lovely to see you. If you're happy with the cut, a short review here [link] helps more than you'd think. No pressure at all.

**Freelancer asking a client for a testimonial:**
> Hi Rob, now that the site's live, could I ask a favour? A short testimonial, two or three sentences, would help me a lot with the next few clients. To make it painless, I've drafted one below you can edit or replace:
> "Nina rebuilt our site in six weeks; it's faster, looks better, and enquiries are up. She was easy to work with and hit every date."
> If you'd rather write your own, even better. Thanks, Rob.

**Asking for a referral:**
> Hi Rob, quick ask: if you know anyone who's in the same spot you were six months ago, I'd be grateful for an intro. No need to sell me, just a "you should talk to Nina". And if nobody comes to mind, no worries at all.

**Asking for a case study (bigger ask):**
> The results on your project were the best I've had this year and I'd love to write it up as a short case study, with your approval on every word. It'd take about 20 minutes of your time on a call. Would you be open to it? Happy to anonymise if that's easier.

**Reminder, once:**
> Just floating this back up in case it got buried: [link]. Totally fine if it's not your thing.

**After a customer leaves a review:**
> Saw your review, thank you so much. It genuinely made my week.

**Responding to "I'll do it later":**
> Thanks! No rush. Here's the link again so it's easy to find: [link].

## Avoid
- "It would mean the world to me" (too much).
- Asking before the product's arrived or the work's done.
- Asking unhappy customers for public reviews (ask them for private feedback instead).
- Multiple platforms in one ask.
- Reminding more than once.
- Offering discounts for reviews where it's against the rules.

## If they ignore it or say no
No reply after the one reminder is a no; drop it and ask the next customer. If they decline outright, thank them and do not ask what stopped them. If they reply with a complaint instead, treat it as a support ticket, not a review request: fix the problem first, and do not send the link again until it is fixed and they are happy.
> Totally understood, thanks for letting me know. Enjoy the lamp.
> Sorry, I didn't know about the flicker. Let me sort that first; could you send a quick photo?
If they leave a lukewarm public review, reply publicly with thanks and one line on what you'll do about it. See `respond-to-negative-review`.
