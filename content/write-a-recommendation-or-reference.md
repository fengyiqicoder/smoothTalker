# Writing a recommendation or reference

## Goal
The reader believes you, because you are specific, and the person you recommend is helped, because the evidence fits the role. A reference is only worth something if it is honest, so the kindest reference is a truthful one with the strongest true things put first.

Asking someone to be your referee: `ask-for-reference-or-recommendation`.

## Structure
1. **How you know them**: role, how long, how closely. This sets how much weight the reader gives you.
2. **Two or three strengths, each with one concrete example.** A number, a project, a moment.
3. **What they would be good at next**, tied to the role or programme they are applying for.
4. **A clear endorsement** at the level you truly mean ("without hesitation", "strongly", "for roles like X").
5. **An offer to talk**, with contact details, for letters.

## Principles
- Specific beats superlative. "She cut our month-end close from nine days to five" does more than "exceptional".
- Ask them for a short brief: the role, the deadline, and two or three things they would like you to mention. Then only write what you can stand behind.
- Describe what you saw. Do not claim to know about work you did not observe.
- Match length to format: LinkedIn is 3 to 5 sentences, a letter is under a page, a reference call is short answers with one example each.
- If you cannot recommend someone, say no to the request early and kindly, rather than writing something lukewarm. A faint reference hurts more than no reference.
- With reservations, narrow the scope instead of lying: recommend them for what they were good at and stay silent or factual on the rest. Never invent strengths, and never hide something serious that a reader has a right to know, such as a safeguarding concern.
- Check your company's policy before giving a reference for a former employee; some allow only dates and title.

## Examples

**LinkedIn recommendation, peer:**
> I worked alongside Priya for three years on the payments team. She's the person everyone went to when an incident got messy: calm, fast, and clear in the incident channel. She rebuilt our alerting so false pages dropped by about 70 percent. Any team would be lucky to have her.

**LinkedIn recommendation, former report:**
> I managed Tom for two years. He joined as a junior analyst and within a year was running our quarterly forecast on his own. What stood out was his judgement: he knew when a number looked wrong and chased it down before it reached the board. I'd hire him again tomorrow.

**Reference letter, opening and close:**
> I'm pleased to recommend Ana Lopes for the MSc in Environmental Policy. I supervised Ana for 18 months as a research assistant at the city council, where she led the data collection for our air quality study across 40 schools...
> I recommend Ana without hesitation, and I'd be glad to talk further: 07700 900456 or j.hart@example.org.

**Reference call, a direct question about a weakness:**
> Early on, she took on too much herself instead of delegating. By her second year she'd fixed that; she was running a team of three and the handoffs were clean.

**Recommending someone for part of what they did (with reservations):**
> I can speak to Sam's client work, which was excellent: he kept our two largest accounts through a difficult year. I worked less closely with him on internal projects, so I'll leave those to others.

**Declining to be a reference, kindly:**
> Thanks for asking me, I'm glad the search is going well. I don't think I'm the best person for this one, since we only worked together briefly on the launch. Someone who saw more of your day-to-day work will give you a stronger reference.

**Nanny or tradesperson, personal reference:**
> Maria looked after our two children for four years. She was reliable to the minute, the kids adored her, and she handled a broken arm at the park with total calm. We recommend her wholeheartedly.

## Avoid
- Superlatives with no evidence ("amazing", "rockstar").
- A generic template with the name swapped in.
- Coded faint praise ("punctual and always well dressed") instead of an honest no.
- Stretching what you saw, or signing a draft they wrote that says things you do not believe.
- Mentioning protected characteristics, health or family circumstances.
- Promising a reference and then missing the deadline.

## If it goes badly
If you realise after agreeing that you cannot write a strong reference, tell them now, privately, and help them find a better referee. If a reference call asks something you cannot answer honestly in their favour, answer briefly and factually, or say it is outside what you saw. If they ask to see what you wrote and you would not be comfortable showing them, rewrite it until you would.
> I've thought about it more, and I don't think I'm the right referee for this role. I only saw a small part of your work. Could Dana be a better fit? I'm glad to tell her what I did see.
> That's outside what I worked on with him, so I can't really speak to it. What I can say is that his client work was consistently strong.
