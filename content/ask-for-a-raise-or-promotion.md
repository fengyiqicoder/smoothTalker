# Asking for a raise or promotion

## Goal
Your manager leaves with a specific number or title, the evidence to defend it upstairs, and a date for an answer. You are writing their case for them.

For negotiating a new job offer, use `negotiate-price-or-salary`.

## Structure
1. **Book the conversation separately.** A short message asking for 20 to 30 minutes about your role and pay. Never ambush a one-to-one or a corridor chat.
2. **Open with commitment.** One line that you like the work and want to grow here.
3. **Evidence, three items maximum.** Outcomes with numbers or scope: what changed because of you, and what you now own beyond your job description.
4. **The ask, as a specific number or title.** Anchored in the role and the market, not your personal costs.
5. **Invite the path.** "If that's not possible now, what would it take and by when?"
6. **Follow up in writing** the same day, with what was agreed and the date for an answer. "I'll get back to you" is fine; "by when?" is the follow-up.

## Principles
- Timing matters more than wording. Best moments: after a clear win, before budget planning, at review season, or when your role has already grown. Worst: the week of a layoff or a bad quarter.
- Ask for a number, not "a bit more". A specific figure (or a salary band level) gives your manager something to take upstairs.
- Market data and scope beat need. "The role now includes managing two people" works; "rent went up" does not, even when it is true.
- A promotion is a title plus scope plus pay. Ask which of the three is on the table, and do not accept the title alone without talking about the other two.
- Never bluff about an outside offer. If you have a real one, you may mention it once, calmly, and only if you would take it.

## Examples

**Booking the meeting (Slack or email):**
> Hi Priya, could we find 30 minutes in the next couple of weeks to talk about my role and compensation? No rush on your side, I just want to give it proper time rather than squeeze it into our 1:1.

**The opening and ask, in the meeting:**
> I really like the work here and I want to keep growing in this team. Over the last year I've taken on the vendor contracts, cut onboarding time from three weeks to one, and I'm now training both new hires. Based on that and what similar roles pay, I'd like to move to £58,000. What would you need from me to make that case?

**Asking for a promotion:**
> I've been doing most of the senior-analyst scope since March: owning the quarterly model and reviewing the juniors' work. I'd like to be considered for the senior title in this cycle. Can we map out what's left to get there?

**Pay gap with peers, factual:**
> I've done some research and my salary looks below the band for this level, based on [source]. I'd like to understand how my pay was set and whether we can bring it in line.

**Follow-up email after the meeting:**
> Thanks for making time today. To confirm: you'll take the move to £58,000 to Marco and come back to me by 15 May. I'll send over the onboarding numbers by Friday in case they help.

**After a "not right now":**
> Understood, thanks for being straight with me. Can we agree the two or three things you'd want to see, and revisit in the September review? I'll put a note in both our calendars.

**When budget is truly frozen:**
> If salary is fixed this year, could we look at a title change, extra leave or a training budget in the meantime, and put the raise first in line for January?

## Avoid
- Comparing yourself to a named colleague ("but Tom earns more").
- Personal-need reasons as the main argument.
- Ultimatums you do not mean.
- Apologising for asking, or opening with "I know it's a bad time, but...".
- Asking by email only. Book the conversation, then confirm it in writing.

## If it goes badly
A no is information, not a verdict. Stay calm, ask what would change the answer, and get the criteria and a review date in writing. If you get a flat no with no path, or the path moves every time you reach it, that tells you the ceiling here: start looking quietly, and do not threaten to leave before you have somewhere to go. If the conversation turns tense, end it kindly and follow up in writing the next day.
> Thanks for being honest. So I'm clear: what would need to be true for this to be a yes, and when can we look at it again?
> I appreciate you raising it with Marco. Since the criteria have shifted since March, could we write down what's needed now so we're both working to the same list?
