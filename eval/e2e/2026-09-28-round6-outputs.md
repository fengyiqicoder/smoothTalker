### S35
**Playbooks chosen:** messages-to-teachers-and-schools (a parent raising a wellbeing concern with the class teacher: facts, a question rather than a verdict, and a meeting)

**Reply to user:**

Write to the teacher first, give the facts as your daughter told them, and ask for a meeting. Don't open with an accusation. That way the teacher can explain or change things without getting defensive, and you'll still have a dated record if you need to take it further. Send it in school hours and keep a copy.

**Warmer**
> Hi [teacher's name], I'm [your name], [daughter's name]'s [mum/dad] ([class]). [Daughter's name] has been very upset about school lately, and she's been crying every morning before she leaves. She's told me she feels embarrassed when her handwriting is commented on in front of the class. **More direct**
> Hi [teacher's name], I'm [your name], [daughter's name]'s [mum/dad] ([class]). For the past [how long], [daughter's name] has been crying every morning before school. She tells me her handwriting has been pointed out and laughed at in front of the class, and it's really affecting her. I'd like to meet to talk about it and agree how her handwriting feedback can be given privately from now on. Could we meet by [day]? I'm free [times].

Use the warmer one if you want to hear the teacher's side first. It's the best way to get a good first meeting. Use the direct one if your daughter has described specific incidents clearly, or if it has been going on for a while, because it asks for a concrete change.

If you get no reply in three school days, send one short follow-up. If the reply is defensive or nothing changes, write to the head of year or the head teacher with the dates of your messages and ask what the school's next steps are. It's also fine to ask for the school's complaints policy.
